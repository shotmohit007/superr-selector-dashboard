      Analysis pipeline

      1 · Rebuild the scoreboardFor every ball: score, wickets, balls left, recent wickets, target and required rate, as they stood before the ball.
      2 · Flag pressureApply the triggers in section 1 to mark each ball as pressure or not.
      3 · Set baselinesAverage outcome on pressure balls for each format, innings, phase, wickets band and role; wicket values; ground factors.
      4 · Score every ballValue = what happened minus what was expected, with wickets priced in runs.
      5 · Rate and rankAdd up per innings, then per player: impact, consistency, recent form. Percentiles, weights, top 10.

      1Pressure definition

      A pressure ball is a delivery bowled when the match situation makes it matter more than usual. We check the scoreboard before each ball, so a good or bad outcome never decides whether the ball counted. Each side of the game has its own situations and indicators:

      Batting first

      • Behind par (score 10%+ below the usual for that over and wickets)
      • Early collapse (3 down in 10 overs, or 2 wickets in 12 balls)
      • Two dot balls in a row
      • Knockout game

      Chasing

      • Required rate 2+ above the current rate
      • 5+ down with 30+ still needed
      • Tight finish: overs 16–20, needing 6–15 an over
      • Early collapse · knockout game

      Bowling first

      • Batters above par
      • Death overs with 5 or fewer down
      • The ball after being hit for 4 or 6
      • Knockout game

      Defending a total

      • Chasers ahead of the required rate
      • Tight finish (as above)
      • The ball after being hit for 4 or 6
      • Knockout game

      Par = the average first-innings score after the same number of legal balls with the same wickets lost (0–1, 2–3, 4–5, 6+), within the format. Chase rules apply only while runs are still needed; rate comparisons start after 2 overs.

      Why these indicators: the side's chance of winning drops

      2Performance measurement

      Metrics

      Batting

      • Runs above expected on pressure balls, with dismissals charged their wicket value
      • Strike rate and dismissals per 100 balls, against expected and against the player's normal balls
      • Dot-ball %, strike rotation (1s, 2s, 3s), balls per boundary
      • After walking in during a collapse: first-10-ball strike rate, dot %, early dismissals, and strike rate afterwards
      • Partnerships, reaching the ground's par score, not out at the end of a won chase

      Bowling

      • Runs saved against expected on pressure balls, with wickets credited their wicket value
      • Economy and wickets per 24 balls, against expected and against the player's normal balls
      • Dot-ball %, boundaries conceded per over
      • Comeback after a 10+ over in their first two overs
      • Overs 19–20 with the game still open: economy and dot %

      Player baselines

      Each player is measured against two baselines:

      • The situation baseline (used in the score): what an average player produced on pressure balls in the same format, innings, phase (overs 1–6, 7–15, 16–20), wickets-lost band (0–2, 3–5, 6+) and, for batters, the same role (openers 1–2, middle order 3–5, finishers 6–8). Groups with fewer than 200 pressure balls borrow from a broader group. Expected runs are scaled by the ground's scoring level: the venue's average first-innings total against the format average.
      • The player's own baseline (shown alongside): the same player's strike rate and dismissal rate, or economy and wicket rate, on their non-pressure balls. This shows whether they rise or fall when it matters (section 4).
      Batter value per ball = (runs − expected runs) − (dismissed − expected dismissal chance) × wicket value Bowler value per ball = (expected runs − runs conceded) + (wicket − expected wicket chance) × wicket value

      What a wicket costs

      A wicket is worth the runs a team is expected to lose from that point, from a Duckworth–Lewis-style curve fitted to the first-innings data. Middle-over wickets with others already down cost the most; a last-over wicket costs very little.

      Rating and scoring logic

      • Pressure innings (or spell): any innings with at least one pressure ball, including quick dismissals.
      • Impact: average value per pressure innings, so every chance counts equally.
      • Consistency: share of pressure innings better than expected.
      • Recent form: impact over the last 12 months of data (needs 3+ pressure innings in that window).

      3Fair comparisons

      • Sample size: a player needs at least 8 pressure innings (or spells) to be ranked, and 3 in the last 12 months for recent form to count. Quick dismissals count as failed chances, not missing ones.
      • Player eligibility: batters at 9–11 are not ranked; bench players and super overs are excluded.
      • Different match contexts: expected values already allow for innings, phase, wickets lost, batting role and the ground's scoring level. Rain-shortened games use the revised overs and target.
      • IPL and T20I data: expected values are worked out within each format, so nobody gains just because the IPL scores more. The final ranking pools both formats; the dashboard can rank each format separately, and every player card shows T20I and IPL impact side by side.
      • Not adjusted: opposition strength. Runs against associate nations count like runs against top sides.

      Where the model is less certain

      • Small samples: players with 8–15 pressure innings, often young players whose whole record is recent, can top the list with wide rank ranges.
      • Broad indicators: the agreed rules flag about half of all balls (). Stricter rules change a couple of names.
      • Missing matches: no T20 World Cup or Champions Trophy games are in the data, so the biggest pressure matches of the period are absent.
      • Missing context: no pitch reports, venue names, weather or reliable ball speeds. Conditions enter only through the ground's scoring level.
      • No opposition adjustment, and team results are shown only as context, because one player rarely decides a match.

      6Reproducibility

      1. Join the six files: matches to competitions on comp_id, deliveries to matches on match_id, player ids to names through the squad table on player_id.
      2. Keep innings 1 and 2. Team runs = runs + both extras columns. Legal ball = not a wide or no-ball. Bowler wickets = caught, bowled, LBW, stumped, hit wicket.
      3. Rebuild the scoreboard before each ball and apply the pressure indicators in section 1.
      4. Fit the wicket-value curve, build the baseline tables and ground factors, and score every pressure ball.
      5. Total ball values per innings, then per player. Apply the 8-innings rule, take percentiles and combine 45 / 30 / 25.
      6. Run build_pressure.py then build_app_data.py (Python 3 with pandas, numpy and scipy). The code is in the appendix.

      Parameters: par margin 10% (min 4 runs) · collapse 3 down in 10 overs or 2 wickets in 12 balls · required-rate gap 2 · tight finish 6–15 an over in overs 16–20 · 200 balls per baseline group · ground factor needs 5 games · 8 pressure innings to rank · 3 for form · 365-day form window · 300 resamples (seed 7).
